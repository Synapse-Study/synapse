## Notes

Pharmacy: the handling of drugs.

Pharmacology/toxicology: the science of drugs (drug design and synthesis, absorption metabolism and excretion of drugs.) Toxicology is a branch of pharmacology dealing with poisons).

Therapeutics: the medical use of drugs (treatment of diseases and its symptoms).

Drug: a chemical substance of a known structure, other than a nutrient or essential dietary ingredient, which, when administered to a living organism, produces a biological effect.

**A good drug can be delivered, stay in the area and carry out an action.**

- Drugs that are used therapeutically typically have at least three names, e.g.:
- Grouped according to therapeutic use, e.g. analgesics, antihypertensives, antibiotics
- Or by mechanism of action, e.g. cyclooxygenase inhibitor, beta-blockers
- *Ibuprofen is a cyclooxygenase inhibitor that acts as an analgesic*


<u>How they work?:</u>

- Drugs exert chemical influence on components of the cell
- Mainly interact with proteins, not really DNA but can be
- They bind to the protein, and alter the function of the protein, by changing its folding


Drugs bind to 4 main proteins: ion channels, carrier molecules (transporters, e.g. SSRIs for depression), receptors and enzymes.

- These proteins can be found in the cell membrane
- or inside the cell

Small drug molecules that bind to large target proteins are called “**ligands**”.

How well it binds to the protein is determined by chemistry, and how well it fits. Most ligands bind reversibly, some irreversibly through covalent interactions. Leads to the formation of a ligand-protein complex that alters the activity of the protein.



For a drug to be useful, it must be <u>selective</u> in its action - a drug can lower blood pressure, but also causes other issues, so not useful.

Can also achieve this by designing a drug that binds to one molecular target and no others - drug is <u>specific</u>

Needs to be both selective and specific.



<u>Pharmacodynamics and Pharmacokinetics:</u>

Pharmacodynamics (PD): what the drug does to the body. E.g. consequences of the drug’s actions at a molecular level, including side effects.

Pharmacokinetics (PK): what the body does to the drug. E.g. how it is handled by the organism, how it is metabolised or how it is distributed.

Both PK and PD need to be considered together - PK/PD studies.



Receptors are important in PD.

Absorption, Distribution, Metabolism, Excretion (ADME) are important in PK.


[IMAGE]

1st pass metabolism is when the drug is broken down in the liver before it goes to the rest of the body. Therefore, drug dosage needs to be controlled to overcome the 1st pass, so the drug actually has an effect.

1. Basic research at uni
1. Identification of potential drug targets
1. Hypothesis generated

Pre-clinical drug discovery

Pre-clinical stage typically takes 5-10 years, with one or two drug candidates taken to clinical trials.

The patent for the new drug lasts 20 years including the pre-clinical stage, after which anyone can copy the drug. Therefore, drug companies are under pressure to get their drug to the market ASAP to recoup costs and get more profit.



Clinical stages:

- Toxicity of the drug will have been tested on at least 2 mammal species
Phase 1:

- Lasts 6 months - 1 year, tests for safety and tolerability
- Involve small number of healthy individuals
- Placebo-controlled, randomised, double-blind
- May involve selected subject groups (male, female, etc.)
Phase 2:

- To determine how clinically effective the drug is in patients, and to confirm safety and tolerability

- Tested on typically 2000-10000 patients
- Lasts several years

- Uses the yellow card reporting system in the UK

# REFERENCE EXAMPLE 3: MCB-13 Molecular Basis of Gene Transcription

## 🎯 Learning Objectives

- Outline the mechanism of RNA synthesis in prokaryotic cells.
- Identify ways in which eukaryotic transcription differs from prokaryotic transcription.
- Describe the co-transcriptional/post-transcriptional modifications that occur in eukaryotic cells during conversion of the primary transcript (heterogeneous nuclear RNA or hnRNA) to mRNA.
- Explain the terms "exon" and "intron".
## 📝 Notes

<b><u>Why transcription matters</u></b>

All cells in the body have the same genomic DNA, so what makes one cell type different from another is which genes get transcribed. In eukaryotes only some of the instructions (mRNAs) are sent out to the cytoplasm, and these differ between cell and tissue types.

<u>Where it happens:</u>

- Eukaryotes: transcription in the nucleus, then the mRNA has to be exported to the cytoplasm before proteins can be made
- Prokaryotes: no nucleus, so transcription and translation both happen in the cytoplasm
This is why prokaryotes don't need to export anything - ribosomes can get started on the mRNA while it is still being made.

Central dogma: DNA is transcribed into RNA, RNA is translated into protein. Transcription is the DNA to RNA bit.



<b><u>DNA vs RNA</u></b>

Three key differences:

- DNA is double stranded, RNA is single stranded (but can fold up into secondary structures - this matters later for the hairpin in termination)
- RNA has ribose, which has an OH at the 2' position. DNA has deoxyribose, which only has an H there. The extra OH makes RNA more reactive and shorter-lived, which suits a temporary copy
- RNA has uracil instead of thymine (bases are ACGU in RNA, ACGT in DNA)


<u>Types of RNA:</u>

- mRNA - encodes proteins
- tRNA - delivers amino acids to the ribosome
- rRNA - structural and enzymatic component of the ribosome
<u>Transcription itself:</u>

Transcription is the production of mRNA from a DNA template. The job of joining ribonucleoside triphosphates into RNA is done by RNA polymerase - they come in through a channel and are joined together in the active site.

<u>Features of a gene:</u>

- Promoter is upstream (5') of the transcription start site
- Termination site is downstream of the start site
- Not all of the mRNA codes for protein - the 5' UTR and 3' UTR at either end don't encode any part of the protein, but contain sequences that regulate translation

(slide 11: key features of a gene) Know the order along the gene: promoter, start site, 5' UTR, coding region, 3' UTR, termination site.



<b><u>Transcription in prokaryotes</u></b>

Three stages: initiation, elongation and termination.

<u>Initiation:</u>

RNA polymerase does have affinity for DNA, but it isn't sequence specific, so on its own it would just stick anywhere. It needs the sigma factor to tell it where to start.

- Short recognition sequences ('boxes') in the promoter align RNA polymerase at the transcription start site
- The two boxes are the -10 box (Pribnow box) and the -35 box
- Sigma factor binds to the -10 and -35 boxes
- Sigma factor then recruits RNA polymerase to the transcription start site

[IMAGE]
(slide 15: promoter with the -10 and -35 boxes)


[IMAGE]

Look at the sequences in the boxes - the quiz on slide 25 asks you to spot them in a real DNA sequence.

<u>Elongation:</u>

- RNA polymerase unwinds the DNA as it moves
- It reads the DNA template and makes mRNA by adding complementary nucleotides
- No primer needed *(unlike DNA polymerase in replication - MCB-12)*
- After the first few nucleotides are done the sigma factor dissociates
- Nucleotides are added 5' to 3'
- Nucleotides are joined by covalent phosphodiester bonds, same as in DNA
Because the new RNA is made 5' to 3', the template strand is read 3' to 5'. This means the mRNA has the same sequence as the non-template (coding) strand, just with U in place of T.


[IMAGE]
(slide 18: transcription bubble) Look at the bubble - the strands are separated inside the polymerase and the new RNA is complementary to the template.

<u>Termination:</u>

Two ways in E. coli: rho independent (the most common) and rho dependent.

Rho independent:

- Controlled by GC-rich stop sequences in the DNA
- The mRNA transcript forms a stem loop (hairpin), which reduces binding of the mRNA to the DNA template
- This is followed by a string of weak A-U pairings, which helps the RNA come away from the template
A-U pairs only have 2 hydrogen bonds (G-C has 3), so they are easy to pull apart.


[IMAGE]

Rho dependent:

- RNA polymerase pauses at certain sequences, often GC-rich, probably because the strands are hard to separate (3 hydrogen bonds between G and C)
- Rho is a protein with helicase activity - it unwinds the DNA-RNA duplex, separating the new RNA from the DNA
- When RNA polymerase stalls, rho 'catches up' with it and releases the RNA from the transcription machinery
Easy way to split them: rho independent is just the hairpin doing the job, rho dependent needs the rho protein.

<b><u>Transcription in eukaryotes</u></b>

Same basic idea (RNA polymerase making RNA from a DNA template, 5' to 3'), but there are more polymerases and the RNA has to be processed before it can leave the nucleus.

<u>RNA polymerases:</u>

Prokaryotes have a single RNA polymerase. Eukaryotes have three:

- RNA Pol I: rRNA
- RNA Pol II: mRNA and snRNA
- RNA Pol III: 5S rRNA and tRNA
Pol II is the one for protein-coding genes. It has a C-terminal domain (CTD) 'tail', which recruits the processing factors (capping, splicing and polyadenylation) to the new mRNA. This matters because only fully processed mRNA can be exported from the nucleus and made available for translation.

Before processing the primary transcript is called heterogeneous nuclear RNA (hnRNA) or pre-mRNA.

*(slide 39 says "heteronuclear RNA" - heterogeneous nuclear is the standard term, check)*

<u>Capping (5' end):</u>

- 7-methylguanosine cap formed at the 5' end
- Guanosine is added by reaction with GTP and joined on by a 5' to 5' triphosphate bridge
- Protects the transcript from degradation, and has roles in export from the nucleus and translation

[IMAGE]

<u>Polyadenylation (3' end):</u>

- Lots of adenosine (A) residues added to the 3' end (50-250 on the slide)
- Not encoded by the DNA template, they are added on afterwards
- Same jobs as the cap: protects from degradation, and has roles in export and translation
Cap is 5', tail is 3' - the quiz tries to swap them round.

<u>Exons and introns:</u>

Eukaryotic genes are split. Exons are the parts that end up in the mature mRNA - they are 'expressed'. Introns are removed from the pre-mRNA and never appear in the mature mRNA. So mature mRNA is made of exons only, it isn't introns with the exons taken out.

<u>Splicing:</u>

The spliceosome removes the introns from the pre-mRNA.

<u>Alternative splicing:</u>

Some pre-mRNAs can be spliced in different ways, using different combinations of exons. This gives different protein products from the same gene, so you can end up with more proteins than genes.

<u>Eukaryotes vs prokaryotes:</u>

- Polymerases: one in prokaryotes, three in eukaryotes
- Location: prokaryotes in the cytoplasm, eukaryotes in the nucleus (mRNA then exported)
- Processing: eukaryotic mRNA is capped, polyadenylated and spliced, which prokaryotic mRNA isn't
- Eukaryotic processing can be co-transcriptional
